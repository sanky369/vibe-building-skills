## 1. The shape of the whole thing

```
FOUNDATION SPRINT (2 days)  →  Founding Hypothesis  →  DESIGN SPRINTS (weeks)  →  "Click"
   Basics → Differentiation → Approach                  build + test prototypes     product-market fit
```

The Foundation Sprint answers **what to build and why**. The Design Sprint (from *Sprint*) answers **does the prototype work** by testing with real customers. You loop Design Sprints, adjusting the hypothesis, until the product clicks. The Foundation Sprint uses **only the team's existing knowledge** — no research or discovery phase — which is why the right people must be in the room. Its speed comes from artificial deadlines that force big decisions fast.

When running this solo with a user, you compress the two days into a focused session, but keep the *sequence* and the *commit-at-each-step* discipline intact.

## 2. Setup

- **Tiny team:** ≤5 people including the **Decider** (the real decision-maker). Solo-with-AI: the user is the Decider; you are facilitator + stand-in team.
- **Time:** two ~6-hour days (≈10 hours). The slack is deliberate — room for breaks and deep focus.
- **Supplies:** whiteboard (real or virtual), sticky notes, blank paper, dot stickers for voting.

## 3. Day 1 Morning — Basics

The "so-obvious-they-get-ignored" fundamentals. Each step uses Note-and-Vote.

| Step | ~Time | Prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Target customer | 15 min | Plain language, real people. Narrow > broad. |
| Customer problem | 15 min | Pain big enough to justify the cost of switching (time/energy/money). What's the biggest help you offer? |
| Your advantage | 20 min | What sets your team far apart? Three dimensions: **capability, insight, motivation.** |
| Competitors | 20 min | All options incl. workarounds and **"do nothing."** Name the **800-pound gorilla** (hardest to beat). |

Notes:
- **"Do nothing" as the top competitor is a warning sign** — the problem may not be painful enough to merit any solution.
- The **advantage** is your unfair edge. *Capability* = what you can do that rivals can't. *Insight* = something true you understand that others don't (often the seed of differentiation). *Motivation* = why you'll push through when others quit.

## 4. Day 1 Afternoon — Differentiation

The crux of the hypothesis. "How can you reframe the decision for customers and make the 800-pound gorilla look like junk?"

| Step | ~Time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Differentiation classics | 20 min | Dot-vote where you could beat the competition on classic spectrums. Be optimistic *and* realistic. |
| Choose your own differentiators | 20 min | Write custom criteria where you'd excel and rivals look crummy. |
| Score custom, part 2 | 10 min | Mark where you stack up vs. competition on the custom criteria. |
| Choose differentiators | 5 min | Decider picks **two** to try first. |
| Make the 2x2 differentiation chart | 30 min | Two differentiators as axes; plot yourself + rivals. Iterate until you're **alone top-right** and rivals fall into **Loserville** (the L of the other three quadrants). Be honest — you must be able to deliver. |
| Write principles | 45 min | Note-and-Vote 2–3 practical principles that deliver the differentiation (e.g., differentiator "fast" → principle "Fast is better than slow"). |
| Mini Manifesto | — | Differentiation + principles on one page. The north star for prototype #1. |

**Classic differentiator spectrums** are simple scales like *fast, easy, free/cheap, simple, focused, premium, fun, trustworthy, flexible, beautiful.* Treat the list as a prompt, not gospel — generate the spectrums that fit the domain. Each is a pole on a scale; you want to credibly own the desirable end *relative to the gorilla*.

**Why a 2x2 here when most differentiation charts are slide-deck filler?** Because this one is about **customer perception** and is tied to a falsifiable promise. It's not decoration — it's the visual form of your hypothesis.

## 5. Day 2 — Approach (Options + Magic Lenses)

A "pre-pivot": generate alternative directions and choose the best *before* committing.

### Morning — Options
| Step | ~Time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| List all possible approaches | 60 min | Note-and-Vote distinct ways to deliver the value (old ideas + new). One-page summary each: what it is, why it's good in one sentence, a doodle of how it works. |
| Label approaches | 5 min | Decider keeps up to **7**; assign each a letter/color to spot patterns later. |

### Afternoon — Magic Lenses
Externalize each decision-perspective as its own 2x2 so you can *see* and compare, instead of holding arguments in your head.

| Step | ~Time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Make classic charts | 15 min | One 2x2 for each of the four classic lenses (below). |
| Place options on each chart | 45 min | Plot one axis at a time; ask the domain expert / Decider for relative placement ("higher or lower than this one?"). Adjust axis labels as needed — defaults are just a start. |
| Make custom 2x2 charts | 45 min | Add 1–3 custom lenses. Most teams end with up to ~7 charts total. |
| Zoom out and review | 10 min | Look for patterns. Does one option win everywhere? Is one lens the most useful? Do any lenses contradict? |
| Decider decides | 5 min | One **Top Bet** + one **Backup Plan**. |

**The four classic lenses** (question each asks + sensible default axes — adjustable):

| Lens | Question it answers | Default axes (low ←→ high) |
|---|---|---|
| **Customer** | Which approach best solves the problem / gives the most customer value? | weakly solves ←→ strongly solves · low value ←→ high value |
| **Pragmatic** | Which is most feasible for *us* — speed and ease to deliver? | hard to build ←→ easy to build · slow ←→ fast to ship |
| **Growth** | Which spreads and scales best? | hard to grow ←→ easy to grow · weak ←→ strong distribution/virality |
| **Money** | Which has the best business / revenue potential? | low revenue potential ←→ high · weak ←→ strong margins/model |

**Custom lens ideas** (pick what fits): *unique to us*, *founder excitement*, *customer pain addressed*, *no advertising required*, *delivers on our mission*, *defensibility*, *time-to-first-value*. You can also re-plot the options on the **Day-1 differentiation 2x2** as a lens — a strong signal when the chosen approach also lands in your differentiation corner.

**Reading the lenses:** the winner is usually the option that repeatedly lands **top-right** across multiple lenses *and* reinforces your differentiation. Contradictions between lenses are not noise — they reveal a real tradeoff to make consciously (e.g., the most growable option is the least profitable).

## 6. The Founding Hypothesis + scorecard

Exact template:

> **If we help [customer] solve [problem] with [approach], they will choose it over [competitors] because our solution is [differentiator 1] + [differentiator 2].**

It's a Mad-Libs sentence that combines **customer + problem + approach + differentiation + competition** into one simple, compelling promise. Simple is the point — if it's not simple, customers won't pay attention.

**The "Prove it!" scorecard.** Each clause is a prediction; turn each into an existential, testable question:
- Right **customer**?
- Right **problem**?
- Right **approach**?
- Will people **really** choose your solution over the competition?
- Do they **care** about your chosen differentiators, and will they believe you're radically better by those criteria?
- Above all: **Does it click?**

Mark the **riskiest** unchecked box — that's what to test first.

## 7. After the sprint — Design Sprints

Run Design Sprints (the 5-day, or compressed, build-and-test loop from *Sprint*) to answer the scorecard questions with real prototypes and real customers. Each week: prototype → test → adjust the hypothesis. Repeat until it clicks. The Foundation Sprint's job is done when you have a sharp hypothesis and know which box to check first.

## 8. Core techniques

### Note-and-Vote (decide fast without groupthink)
1. **Note (silent, ~5 min):** everyone writes options alone, on separate notes.
2. **Vote (silent):** review in silence; dot-vote the best.
3. **Decider decides:** short debate, then the Decider chooses — **regardless of the vote count.** Votes inform; they don't bind.

This beats open brainstorming because it removes anchoring, status effects, and the loudest-voice problem. **Solo-with-AI adaptation:** you generate the spread of distinct candidates (playing the silent team), the user reacts/adds, the user-as-Decider commits to one. Crucially, *don't anchor on the user's first phrasing* — produce real alternatives so the hidden/better answer can surface.

### Work Alone Together
Individuals think independently *and then* combine — the productive middle between solo work and a meeting. Seek **clarity, not consensus**; the Decider breaks ties.

### Portable 2x2 / Loserville notation
You can't draw, so represent a 2x2 as axes + quadrant placements:

```
Y axis: [low label] ←→ [high label]
X axis: [low label] ←→ [high label]
WIN = top-right ([high X] + [high Y])

| Quadrant            | Who lands here     | Read        |
| Top-right (win)     | YOU                | own it      |
| Top-left            | Competitor A       | Loserville  |
| Bottom-right        | Competitor B       | Loserville  |
| Bottom-left         | "do nothing"       | Loserville  |
```

"Loserville" = the L-shaped three non-winning quadrants. The test of a good chart: **you are alone in the top-right and can actually deliver it.** If two players share the corner, sharpen the axes.

## 9. The deeper lessons (carry these into every evaluation)

- **It either clicks or it doesn't.** Optimize for a simple promise people want and will repeat, not for feature count.
- **The obvious is not obvious.** Three smart cofounders give three different "target customers." Writing the basics down *is* the work.
- **Differentiation is the strongest predictor of success** — and it must be radical and deliverable, not a tagline.
- **Make the implicit explicit.** A hypothesis you can see is a hypothesis you can test and kill cheaply. Knapp wasted two years on a 3D virtual conference room because the bad hypothesis stayed hidden; one week on "the fastest, easiest video call" became Google Meet.
- **Pre-pivot.** Generate and compare directions on paper before building. Pivoting on a sticky note is free.
- **In the age of AI, deciding what to build is the edge.** Anyone can build fast; differentiation and a clear hypothesis are how you stand out in a noisier world. "Building nothing first" — getting the bet right — often reaches product-market fit faster than rushing to code.
- **Speed and quality aren't opposites here.** Tight time limits force the clarity that endless meetings never produce.
