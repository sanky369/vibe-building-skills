# Foundation Sprint

A method for starting a project the smart way: get clear on **who** you serve, **what** problem you solve, **how** you're radically different, and **which** approach to try first — then state it as one testable bet and prove it. Adapted from *Click: How to Make What People Want* (Jake Knapp & John Zeratsky, Character Capital). The original is a 2-day team workshop; here it runs as a fast, structured thinking session between you and the user.

**Prime directive: the only question that matters is "Does it click?"** A product clicks when it makes a *simple, compelling promise* that customers want, that the product delivers, and that they tell others about. Everything below exists to find that promise and expose where it might be wrong.

## When to use it

| Mode | Trigger | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Evaluate / start** | A new idea, "should I build this?", "is this good?", choosing a direction | Produce a sharp, testable **Founding Hypothesis** + scorecard |
| **B — Improve** | An existing product, "how do I make this better?", stalled growth, weak differentiation | Reverse-engineer the *current* hypothesis, find the weakest prediction, fix it |

If it's unclear which, default to A. For B, see "Mode B" near the end.

## Operating principles (these shape every output)

1. **Clarity is the bottleneck, not creativity.** The "basics" are so obvious they get skipped — and that's exactly why teams misalign. Ask three founders who the customer is and you get three answers. Force them into the open.
2. **Differentiation is the #1 predictor of success.** Aim for *radical*, not incremental. The goal is to make the strongest competitor (the "800-pound gorilla") look like junk for this customer. In an age where anyone can build anything fast with AI, *what* you build and *how you stand out* matter more than execution speed.
3. **Drag hidden predictions into the spotlight.** Every idea secretly assumes a customer, a problem, an approach, and a reason people will switch. Name them. You may not like what you see (that's the point).
4. **Simple promises win.** If the promise isn't simple, customers won't pay attention. "More storage + great search" beat every other inbox. Compress, don't accumulate features.
5. **Be the Decider, not a menu.** At every convergence point, commit to **one** choice with a one-line reason. Offer a single backup, never an option buffet. (This is the book's "Decider decides" rule.)
6. **Honesty over optimism.** "Differentiation only works if you can actually deliver it." Be aggressive about where you *could* win, then ruthless about whether you can keep the promise.
7. **Speed paradox: decide before you build.** This whole exercise is a "pre-pivot" — pivot on paper, cheaply, before writing code.

## How to run it

First, gather just enough. Ask the user (in one tight batch, only what's missing): **the idea in a sentence, who it's for, and what people use today instead.** If the idea is already well-specified, skip the questions, state your assumptions explicitly, and proceed — don't stall.

Then run four phases. For each *divergent* step, **don't accept the first answer.** Generate 3–5 genuinely distinct candidates yourself (you're standing in for a whole team — surface the non-obvious ones), invite the user to react or add, then have the user-as-Decider commit to one. This solo "Note-and-Vote" is what prevents lazy, mode-collapsed answers.

Use plain language throughout ("teams in different cities," not "distributed enterprise orgs"). If a market claim matters and is checkable, verify it with a quick search and cite it; otherwise label it an assumption.

### Phase 1 — Basics
Lock down four fundamentals (commit to one each):
- **Target customer** — real, specific people. Narrow beats broad.
- **Customer problem** — painful enough to justify the time/money/effort of switching. What's the *biggest* way you help?
- **Your advantage** — your unfair edge, across three dimensions: **capability** (what you can do that others can't), **insight** (what you understand that others don't), **motivation** (why you'll outlast others). At least one must be strong.
- **Competitors** — every option the customer has, *including workarounds and "do nothing."* If "do nothing" is the real competitor, the problem may not be painful enough — flag that. Name the **800-pound gorilla**: the hardest-to-beat option.

### Phase 2 — Differentiation
This is the crux. Find the axes on which you can be radically better *for this customer*.
- **Score classic differentiators.** Rate where you could beat the gorilla on common spectrums (e.g., fast, easy, simple, cheap/free, focused, premium, fun, trustworthy). Be optimistic *but* realistic.
- **Add custom differentiators** — criteria specific to your insight where you'd shine and the competition looks crummy.
- **Pick the two** you'll lead with (the Decider chooses).
- **Build the differentiation 2x2.** Put your two differentiators on the axes. Plot yourself and each competitor. Keep adjusting until **you sit alone in the top-right** and the competition is pushed into the other three quadrants — the L-shaped "Loserville." If you can't get alone in the corner honestly, your differentiation isn't sharp enough yet. (Portable format for the 2x2 is in `references/method.md`.)
- **Write 2–3 principles** that operationalize the differentiation (if "fast" is a differentiator, a principle is "Fast is better than slow"). These guide later build decisions.
- **Mini Manifesto** = the two differentiators + the principles on one page. This is the north star for the first prototype.

### Phase 3 — Approach (the "pre-pivot")
*How* you deliver the value — there's always more than one path.
- **Generate approaches.** List several distinct ways to deliver (different formats: product, service, tool, marketplace, content, concierge, AI-native, etc.). Pick up to ~7 and label each.
- **Evaluate with Magic Lenses.** Externalize each perspective as its own 2x2 so you can *see* the tradeoffs instead of juggling them. Start with the four classics, then add 1–3 custom lenses (so you usually end with up to ~7 charts total):
  - **Customer lens** — which approach best solves the problem / delivers most value to the customer?
  - **Pragmatic lens** — which is most feasible for *you* (speed and ease to deliver)?
  - **Growth lens** — which spreads and scales best (distribution, virality)?
  - **Money lens** — which has the best business/revenue potential?
  - **Custom lenses** — e.g., *unique to us*, *founder excitement*, *no advertising required*, *delivers on our mission*; you can also re-plot options on the Phase-2 differentiation 2x2.
  Plot the same options on every lens. (Lens definitions + default axes + how to read them: `references/method.md`.)
- **Zoom out and look for the pattern.** Does one approach keep landing top-right across lenses? Do any lenses contradict each other (an important signal)?
- **Decide:** one **Top Bet** (try first) + one **Backup Plan** (pivot-to if the bet fails). Prefer the approach that lands top-right *and* delivers on your differentiation.

### Phase 4 — Founding Hypothesis + Scorecard
Assemble everything into one Mad-Libs sentence (the book's exact template):

> **If we help [customer] solve [problem] with [approach], they will choose it over [competitors] because our solution is [differentiator 1] + [differentiator 2].**

Then attack it. Every clause is a hidden prediction; turn each into a "Prove it!" question and name the single riskiest assumption — that's what to test *first*.

## Required output format

Always deliver this structure (markdown, concise):

```
## Founding Hypothesis
> If we help **[customer]** solve **[problem]** with **[approach]**,
> they will choose it over **[competitors]** because our solution is **[diff 1] + [diff 2]**.

## Basics
| Element | Decision | Why this one |
| Customer | ... | ... |
| Problem | ... | ... |
| Advantage | capability / insight / motivation | ... |
| 800-lb gorilla | ... | ... |

## Differentiation 2x2
- Axes: X = [low←→high label] · Y = [low←→high label]
- Win quadrant (top-right): **you** — [why]
- Loserville: [competitor → quadrant], ...
- Principles: 1) ... 2) ...

## Magic Lenses
| Approach | Customer | Pragmatic | Growth | Money | [custom] | Pattern |
| A ... | win | ... | ... | ... | ... | wins 4/5 |
(score each cell win / mid / weak = where it lands on that lens's 2x2)
- **Top Bet:** [approach] — [one-line reason]
- **Backup:** [approach]

## Prove-it Scorecard
| Prediction | "Prove it!" test | Risk |
| Right customer? | [cheapest test] | H/M/L |
| Right problem? | ... | ... |
| Right approach? | ... | ... |
| Real differentiation? (will they switch, do they care) | ... | ... |
| **Does it click?** | [the make-or-break test] | ... |

## First move
The single riskiest assumption is **[X]**. Test it next by **[cheapest experiment — a Design Sprint, fake-door, landing page, 5 customer convos, concierge MVP]** before building.
```

Keep it honest and specific. End with the first move, not a summary.

## Mode B — improving an existing product

Same machinery, run in reverse:
1. **Reverse-engineer the current Founding Hypothesis** from how the product actually behaves today (you can usually infer all five clauses). Show it back to the user — misalignment here is the most common root cause.
2. **Run the scorecard against reality.** Which prediction is failing? Weak differentiation, wrong customer, a problem that isn't painful enough, or an approach the market rejected?
3. **Re-sharpen the failing clause.** Usually it's differentiation: rebuild the 2x2 and find a corner you can own now (markets, competitors, and your advantage all move). If the *approach* is the problem, re-run Magic Lenses on new options — that's a deliberate pivot.
4. **Output the revised hypothesis + the one change to test first.** Commit to a single highest-leverage move, not a backlog.

## Guardrails

- **Commit, don't enumerate.** One Top Bet, one Backup. No 5-option menus — that defeats the Decider discipline and just relocates the user's indecision.
- **Don't invent market facts.** Mark inferences as assumptions; verify checkable claims with a quick search and cite them.
- **Differentiation must be deliverable.** If you can't honestly own the top-right corner, say so — a beautiful 2x2 you can't deliver is a lie.
- **This replaces research with the team's existing knowledge, not with certainty.** The hypothesis is a *bet to test*, never proof. The next step is always an experiment.
